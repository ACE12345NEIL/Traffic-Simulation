Disturbance Propagation & Shockwave Analysis

A numerical stability analysis investigating whether a localized vehicle slowdown dissipates, remains bounded, or forms an upstream-propagating phantom traffic jam (backward shockwave).

Key Finding:

At densities below critical density (ρ < 0.15), minor perturbations dissipate within 5–10 steps as downstream headway allows vehicles to accelerate. Near and above capacity (ρ ≥ 0.25), a momentary slowdown cannot be absorbed, propagating backward at characteristic speed w ≈ -15 to -18 km/h (-1 cell/step), confirming classical Lighthill-Whitham-Richards (LWR) shockwave dynamics reproduced in the cellular automaton.

Model Architecture & Methodology Comparison

Direct comparison between the benchmark Nagel–Schreckenberg (1992) model and the improved Mumbai arterial model.

Feature Original NaSch (1992) Extended Mumbai Model Modelling Rationale
Lattice Topology Single-lane circular ring Two-lane open arterial (or circular) Represents multi-lane urban arterial corridors with inflow and outflow.
Vehicle Classes Homogeneous (identical cars) Heterogeneous (Cars & Two-Wheelers) Two-wheelers constitute over 40–60% of Mumbai's vehicular composition.
Speed & Acceleration v_max = 5, uniform accel (+1) Configurable per vehicle type Two-wheelers accelerate quickly and have lower dawdling probabilities in dense traffic.
Lane Discipline None (single lane) Strong, Moderate, or Weak Quantifies lane adherence vs. opportunistic lane-filtering and swerving.
Traffic Signals None (uninterrupted flow) Stop-line with Green/Red cycles Accounts for signalized arterial bottlenecks and cyclic queue formation.
Queue Dynamics Phantom jams only Signal queues + backward shockwaves Enables measurement of maximum queue spillback and cycle clearance delay.
Boundary Conditions Periodic (circular) Finite open road with demand inflow Enables realistic trip travel-time and throughput measurement.
